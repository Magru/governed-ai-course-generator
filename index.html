Governed AI Course Generator

Governed AI course generation for an enterprise learning platform: from an empty brief to a published course, with formal layers at every step and the editor at the wheel.

The model proposes. The architecture decides. The editor drives.

Nothing generated reaches a learner because it looked convincing. It reaches them because it was grounded in the organization's own knowledge, permitted by policy, consistent with everything already approved, and signed off by a person.

Domain: Sylla LXP — a production platform Formal stack: OPA · Z3 · Prolog · Datalog · LTL Neural: multi-model, policy-routed Deliverable: standalone prototype

Section 1

Context

Sylla is a production learning experience platform I work on. Organizations run employee training on it: courses, learning paths, assessments, a per-organization skills catalog, a Knowledge Base of internal documents, role- and data-level permissions, and human approvals. Courses are built by hand in a block editor — sections, lessons, and typed blocks (text, media, quizzes); adaptive branching is planned per the builder specification and is not in the codebase today.

The generator described here is a standalone prototype on that domain. It does not touch production code; it models the same entities and the same permission rules, and it is designed so its architecture can graduate into the product.

Section 2

The problem and what success means

Authoring is the most expensive part of the platform — days per course. Generating that content with a model is the obvious move, and an ungoverned generator is a product risk rather than a hypothetical one:

  • it invents skills the organization's catalog does not contain, so the course teaches outside the company's competency model;
  • claims land in lessons with no traceable source;
  • an author's free-form prompt can request something the organization forbids;
  • content can be published without review;
  • a course lives across sessions, so editing one approved lesson silently desynchronizes the exam that tests it;
  • a generator with Knowledge Base access can pull restricted material into a course seen by an audience without rights to it;
  • a course structure can be broken in ways only a graph check catches — prerequisite cycles, unreachable material, an exam that tests a lesson nobody reaches.

Users: course authors and training administrators. Cost of a wrong action: unverified, stale, or restricted content delivered to an entire workforce.

Success: authoring drops from days to hours — with zero courses published that bypassed the formal checks or human approval, zero leaks of restricted sources, and zero published courses carrying desynchronized nodes.

Section 3

Governing principle

One sentence governs every decision that follows:

The model may propose anything. It may decide nothing. Publication, state transitions, and access to sources are decided by the architecture — formal layers first, a person last.

The rest of this specification is the consequence: where proposals are checked, who may say yes, what happens when the answer is no, and how any of it can be reconstructed six months later.

Section 4

Division of responsibility

Three kinds of judgment run this system, and they are not interchangeable. Assigning every question to the layer that can actually answer it is the core architectural decision of the project.

Formal engines

Structure

Rights, ordering, dependencies, arithmetic, coverage, staleness. Deterministic versioned rules that produce a proof, not an opinion.

“A restricted source must not reach a broad-audience course” — a graph of relations.
Managed services

Meaning & modality

Forbidden topics phrased indirectly, harmful text, personal data, what is inside a generated image, whether a retrieved passage is relevant. Configured and versioned outside the application.

“The author asks for something forbidden, in other words” — meaning, not structure.
The editor

Judgment

Whether this is worth teaching, in this order, to these people. Approval of the skeleton, of every node, and of publication.

Delegated to no model, no service, and no rule.

The boundary rule. If a check can be stated about a graph, a set, an ordering, or a number — it belongs to the formal engines. If answering it requires understanding a text or looking at an image — it is configured, never coded. If it is about pedagogical value — a person decides.

Semantics are configuration

Forbidden topics, content thresholds, and multimodal guardrails live in a managed service with its own version history. A compliance officer changes them: no developer, no deployment, no downtime. Their verdict enters the system as a fact for the formal layer — it decides nothing on its own — and if the service is unreachable the gateway fails closed.

Vocabulary is data

The block catalog — every block type, its settings schema, its modality, its applicability policy — is read from the platform at runtime and never copied into the generator. A new modality (video, audio, an interactive block) arrives as a catalog entry: the generator proposes it, its schema validates it, provenance traces it, policy constrains it. Zero code, zero deployments. The same holds for the skills catalog, the Knowledge Base categories with their access rules, thresholds, and the model routing table — organizational data, not constants inside a release.

→ Configuration — detailed specification: the seven sources read at runtime, their owners and record shapes, how a version stamp binds a decision to the rules that made it, what is deliberately left as code, and which of it exists in the platform today.

Section 5

The editor's journey

A course is not generated in one pass. It is a governed journey in three phases, and the expensive work starts only after a human has approved the cheap part.

Phase 1

Skeleton

The author gives a brief; the model proposes modules, lessons, topics, and exams — titles and learning objectives only. Checked formally, then edited and approved by a person before any content is paid for.

Phase 2

Content, over many sessions

Node by node: retrieval scoped to that topic, blocks assembled against the catalog's schemas, checks, repair, approval. State is persistent — the editor leaves and comes back.

Phase 3

Publication

Every node approved and none stale → whole-course checks for completeness, ordering, and consistency → a final human approval → published.

→ Walkthrough — one course, end to end: the same three phases as twenty-four numbered steps, with the state after each one, the three refusals available before the first token, and what still reaches the course once it is published.

Section 6

State model

The lifecycle is an explicit two-level state machine, not a pipeline. The course machine survives editor sessions and aggregates the state of its nodes; each node — lesson, topic, exam — runs its own cycle of drafting, guardrail screening, checks, repair, and approval.

Three properties make it more than bookkeeping. Violations are split by class: a policy violation is terminal, while a constraint or grounding violation is recoverable and enters a bounded repair loop. Approval is honest: editing an approved node withdraws its approval and, through the dependency graph, the approval of everything derived from it. And a published course is immutable — what everyone calls editing a live course forks a new revision beside it, so the cascade never runs against content a learner is reading, and rollback is a pointer move rather than a recovery procedure. A learner who has already started finishes on the revision they started; the new one serves everyone who starts after. The single exception is a revision withdrawn for violating a rule — there access is cut, because “let them finish” is precisely what the compliance officer was preventing.

The state machine is the editor's interface. In every state the system says what is available, what is blocked and why, and what to do next — the next best action is derived from uncovered objectives and stale nodes, not suggested by a model. Re-validation is a gate rather than a notification: publication is unavailable while anything is stale, with the affected nodes listed beside it. That is not a UX preference — the invariant already forbids publishing a stale course, and the gate is that invariant made visible.

→ State model — detailed specification: every course and node state, the event catalog, guards, temporal invariants, and the fields each artifact persists.

Section 7

What the model is given, and how much of it is trusted

Every prompt the gateway sends is assembled from three inputs with three different trust levels, kept separate rather than concatenated:

  1. System instructions — trusted, ours. The generation rules for this phase and a strict output schema, enforced as a tool call so the model returns a structure rather than prose. Each phase has its own: skeleton, node content, illustration.
  2. The author's prompt — authorized, but untrusted. The author steers style and emphasis; the author cannot lift a rule. “Generate without checks” is refused at the entrance, with an explanation, and the model is never called.
  3. Retrieved sources — untrusted data. Passages from the Knowledge Base are quoted as data, never executed as instructions. An instruction embedded in an internal document is inert by construction. Sources are also screened on admission for personal data and instruction-shaped content — not because the quoting is unsafe, but so that an attempt is recorded rather than silently neutralized.

The model's output stays untrusted until it has passed every layer: the schema binds its shape, grounding binds its claims to sources, policy binds the action it implies, and the guardrail screens its meaning. Nothing about it is acted on before those return.

Three properties of the gateway matter more than its individual steps. It is explicit — the guardrail is a call the architecture makes, visible in the state machine and in the trace, not a flag hidden inside a model invocation. It fails closed — if any layer cannot answer, nothing is admitted. And a refusal always carries a safe next action, which is what turns a block into a repair instead of a dead end.

Section 8

The formal stack

Five engines, each owning exactly one kind of question. No check is evaluated twice by two tools.

Z3Arithmetic & satisfiabilityWhether the brief is satisfiable at all — proven before any generation spend, with the contradicting requirements named

Section 9

The neuro-symbolic junction

Only the neural side can write coherent teaching material, invent exam questions, decompose a subject, and illustrate it. Only the symbolic side can prove the result is grounded, permitted, and consistent — and explain a refusal.

They meet in four places:

  1. Grounding — every claim is bound to the skills catalog or a source passage; unbound content is rejected. Symbolic filters neural.
  2. Repair — the formal explanation (a contradiction core, a denial reason, a proof tree) is fed into the next prompt and the model fixes its own output against it. Symbolic steers neural.
  3. Explanation — refusals are explained by derivation, never by “the model decided”. Symbolic justifies.
  4. Process — what the editor should do next is inferred from coverage and staleness, not proposed by a model. Symbolic drives.
Section 10

Governance guarantees

Seven properties the system owes the organization — each enforced structurally rather than promised.

GuaranteeHow it is enforced
Restricted knowledge never leaks Audience rights become a metadata filter on retrieval, and every returned source is re-checked against the permission closure afterwards. The filter makes it fast; the re-check makes it provable.
A rule change never requires a shutdown Every approved artifact is stamped with the policy, guardrail, and catalog versions it passed under. When a rule changes, the dependency graph marks exactly the affected courses for re-validation — a rule change is an event in the system, not a release.
Nothing changes under a learner A published revision is immutable, so a learner finishes on the revision they started — within a bounded window, and told only when a change lands ahead of them — never a banner, never a silent rewrite. And because nothing is edited, rollback is a pointer move to an earlier revision that still exists and still carries the approvals and versions it was cleared under: nothing regenerated, nothing restored from a backup. The same applies one level up — policies, prompts, and catalogs are versioned, so reverting one re-validates exactly what that version touched rather than being a migration.
Every action is reconstructable One continuous trace per course: prompts, model and version, guardrail verdicts, each layer's decision, human approvals with what the approver was shown, and the final outcome.
Side effects happen at most once Generation and publication carry idempotency keys. A timeout is treated as unknown, never as failure: status is checked before any retry. Where an action half-completes, the registry names its compensation up front — publication is undone by moving the pointer, a generated node by discarding a draft revision, and the one action that cannot be taken back, notifying learners, is therefore compensated by a correction notice and an incident record rather than pretended away.
The model never holds credentials Secrets stay with the executor and never enter a prompt, a context, or an open audit record; outputs are scanned for leaked secrets before admission.
Autonomy is earned, and revocable The generator climbs an explicit ladder — propose only, dry run, read-only actions, low-risk writes with approval, high-risk writes with policy and audit. A kill switch disables an action class, a model, or the whole generator without touching the platform.

Actions are registered rather than implicit: retrieval, node generation, image generation, writing into course state, publication, and learner notification each carry a risk level and the controls that level requires. Adding a new action means classifying it first.

Section 11

Loop discipline

The generator is a closed loop, and closed loops have failure modes of their own. Four rules keep it bounded:

  • Repair, not retry. A failed check does not re-run the same request — it returns a specific explanation, and the next attempt is aimed at it. Repeating a request without new information is pointless by construction.
  • Every loop has a stop. Bounded attempts per node, a minimum improvement between rounds, an overall timeout, and escalation to the editor when the budget is spent. A loop that cannot stop is a safety defect, not an inconvenience.
  • Self-critique is not verification. The model may flag its own weaknesses; it may not clear itself. Only the formal layers and the editor approve, and the approval path is separate from the generation path.
  • No refinement without a ruler. Where there is no explicit criterion and no independent verifier, the system escalates instead of iterating. Endless polishing costs money, dilutes good content, and disguises the absence of a real check.

The prototype also keeps no cross-course memory. Anything learned from one course influencing another would need its own retention, provenance, and review policy — deliberately outside this scope rather than quietly present.

Section 12

Content quality

“Generated quickly” is not “publishable”. Quality is enforced per node, as content lands, at three levels:

  1. Structural, formal. Every lesson has objectives; every objective is covered; every exam question traces to material already taught and approved; prerequisites form a coherent chain. A structurally weak node never reaches the editor — it goes to repair.
  2. Substantive, grounded. Content comes from the organization's Knowledge Base and skills catalog. An unsourced claim is a blocking violation — which removes the main source of low quality: fabrication.
  3. Editorial, human. The skeleton is approved before generation, each node is approved with its explanation, and publication is a separate decision. Editing an approved node withdraws its approval, so silent degradation is architecturally impossible.
Section 13

Evaluation

The system is graded on what it refuses as much as on what it produces.

  • Layer tests — each engine in isolation, plus valid and forbidden transitions of both state machines.
  • Forbidden chains — content before an approved skeleton; publication with a stale node; an exam ahead of its material; anything admitted without a guardrail check.
  • Adversarial scenarios — a forbidden request phrased indirectly; an instruction embedded in a source document; a broad-audience course reaching a restricted source; an unsafe illustration; a timeout followed by a retry; an unregistered action proposed.
  • Trajectory evaluation — the path is graded, not only the artifact: which actions ran, in what order, at what cost, and how many repair rounds it took.
  • Dry run — a whole course generated in simulation, reporting what would be produced and what would be blocked, with no side effects.
  • Recorded fixtures — model responses, guardrail verdicts, and retrieval results are committed, so every result above reproduces offline; a live run is recorded separately.
  • Production signals — blocked generations, policy denials, injection detections, near-miss source leaks, repair-loop depth, and the size of the re-validation queue.
Section 14

Technology

  • Models: multi-model on AWS Bedrock, as in production — a strong reasoning model for the skeleton and for repairs, a light one for node text and utility work, an image model for illustrations. Routing is a policy table, not a model's choice, and it carries the rule that restricted sources may only be processed by the organization-approved model. Every artifact records which model and version produced it; each model has a fallback chain.
  • Formal engines are real: OPA (Rego), Z3, SWI-Prolog, a Datalog engine, and an LTL checker over the trace — the same stack I have already run end to end in an earlier assignment.
  • Managed services for guardrails and retrieval, reached through ports so the implementation stays replaceable.
  • Persistent course state (JSON/SQLite): sessions resume and the trace stays continuous across months.
  • Scope: a fixed small course — two modules, a handful of nodes, one exam, a couple of images — with the effort spent on verification depth rather than feature breadth. The editor surface is a wizard rendered from the state machine, not a full UI.
  • Hygiene: secrets outside the repository, configuration separated from code, and versioning for policies, prompts, and state definitions.
Section 15

Limits, stated honestly

What the prototype actually runs. The five formal engines, the state machines, the gateway and the trace are real and run on every pass. Retrieval and model calls go through an adapter over recorded fixtures, so the behaviour under test is deterministic and reviewable — with one genuinely live end-to-end run demonstrated at the defense. The point of the project is the architecture that decides, not the provider that generates; making the demo depend on a network call would risk the former to prove the latter.

  • Pixels are not formally verifiable. An image passes guardrail and provenance checks; the visual itself is a human decision, and the architecture says so rather than pretending otherwise. Block types that need an eye are marked as such in the catalog, and a node cannot be approved while one of them is unseen — so the requirement travels with the vocabulary instead of being wired into the pipeline.
  • A managed guardrail is no judge of pedagogy. It knows nothing about coverage, dependencies, or the organization's permission model — those never leave the formal layer.
  • A formal guarantee is only as good as the model of the world. If the catalog or the permission closure is wrong, the proof is about the wrong thing — which is why both are read from the platform rather than restated.
  • Every extra hop costs latency and money. Checks sit at generation boundaries, verdicts are cached by content hash, and cheap tasks go to the light model.
Section 16

Open questions

  1. Over-refusal is invisible in a way under-refusal is not. A course that reaches an audience it should not have is a detectable failure; a course refused for a rule that should not have applied simply never gets made, and nobody files a report about it. Every guarantee on this page is about the first kind. The evaluation strategy has no equivalent instrument for the second.
  2. What actually blocks lifting this into the product? The prototype models the platform's rules on its own domain deliberately. Three things would have to become real for the architecture to move in: the catalog served at runtime, a managed guardrail in the generation path, and a policy permission distinct from general administration. None is hard; none has been scoped.
  3. The sweep is bounded but unmeasured. A reader grace window caps how far a policy change has to reach, which turns an open-ended cost into a number an organization chooses. What that number costs at real volumes is still a guess.
