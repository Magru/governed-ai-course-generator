What lives in configuration

The generator's vocabulary, its notion of meaning, its limits and its models are organizational data read at runtime — not constants inside a release. This page says exactly which data, in what shape, owned by whom, and what happens the moment any of it changes.

If a rule can change without a developer, it is data. If it changes only with a release, it is code — and it had better be an invariant worth freezing. This page is the inventory of the first kind; Section 9 is the deliberate inventory of the second.

Section 1

The boundary test

The specification's division of responsibility states the rule; this page is its consequence, written out as records. Three questions decide where anything belongs:

  1. Does it differ between organizations? Two customers with different compliance regimes must be able to disagree about it without forking the product. If yes — data.
  2. Must it change without a deployment? A compliance officer who has to file a ticket and wait for a release is a compliance officer who will instead disable the feature. If yes — data.
  3. Does answering it require understanding a text or looking at an image? Then it is not merely data, it is managed data: configured in a service that owns the judgment, with its own version history. Hand-writing that judgment as code is the mistake this whole page exists to avoid.

The failure this prevents has a name. A banned-topic list written as string matching inside the application is the canonical bad guardrail: it is bypassed by paraphrase, it cannot see images, it needs a developer and a deploy to change, and it leaves no version history to audit. Everything in Section 5 exists because that is the tempting way to build it.

Section 2

The first three are not ours to invent — they exist, they have owners, and the generator's job is to consume them rather than grow its own copy. That is the whole argument of the next two sections.

Section 3

Vocabulary — blocks and skills

The block catalog exists, as code

Today the platform holds one exhaustive row per block type in a compile-time registry: category, whether the learner can answer it, whether it needs a live backend, whether the Knowledge Base offers it, whether it is edited in a modal. The set the Knowledge Base exposes is derived from those rows rather than hand-listed, so a new block type cannot be added without deciding where it belongs — the row will not compile without the flag. Backend validators mirror the same set, and a test guards the parity between the two.

source: the platform's block-metadata registry and its derived Knowledge-Base subset

That design is sound for two consumers who share a compile step. The generator is a third consumer, and it does not share one. A copy of the block list inside the generator would be a third mirror of a set that already needs a test to stay in sync twice — and mirrors drift silently, in the direction of the copy nobody edits. So the catalog is read at runtime, and three fields are added to what a row carries:

{ "type": "image", "category": "image", // exists today "interactive": false, // exists today — the learner does not answer it "system": false, // exists today "kb": true, // exists today "content_modal": true, // exists today "modality": "image", // added: what a guardrail must inspect "requires_visual_review": true, // added: gates NodeApproved "settings_schema": "catalog://schemas/image@3", "applicability": { "surfaces": ["course", "kb"] }, "version": 3 }

One caveat before that becomes a slogan: a catalog read at runtime is metadata the generator trusts, and trusted metadata is a supply chain. Entries are authored inside the platform, reviewed and versioned like policy — the registry is never imported from an external source, and a catalog change is stamped and swept exactly like a policy change.

The consequence is the one worth defending: a new modality is a catalog entry, not a release. Video arrives as a row; the generator may propose it, its schema validates it, its modality tells the guardrail what kind of thing to inspect, its review flag decides whether a person must look. No branch in the generator says the word “video”.

The skills catalog exists

Skills, their categories, the links from skills to learning items, and qualifications with their team assignments are real models in the platform. The generator does not extend them and does not cache them — it reads them as facts, and the Datalog check in step 6 is exactly the question “is this claimed skill derivable from these facts?”.

source: the platform's skills models — skill, skill-to-item link, qualification, team qualification

This is what makes “the model invented a skill” a detectable event rather than an editorial opinion.

Section 4

Sources and who may see them exists

The Knowledge Base already has a single-source-of-truth visibility predicate, and it is more subtle than a permission flag. An article is visible if its chain is unrestricted, or the viewer's team is named on the article, or the viewer holds an individual grant on that article, or the viewer's team is inherited from the category tree, or the viewer holds a grant on the category, or they authored it, or they are a local admin — seven clauses, and the two grant clauses are genuinely different rules. Team restrictions inherit downward through the category tree via a materialized closure; individual grants do not — a person granted a category sees that category and its direct articles, not its subcategories.

source: the platform's Knowledge-Base ACL module and its materialized category-team closure

The generator must not re-implement this. It would be the block-catalog mistake with worse consequences: the copy that drifts would be the one deciding who sees restricted material. The permission check in step 12 asks the platform's own predicate, over the same closure, and treats the answer as a fact.

What the generator adds is a direction, not a rule: the platform asks “may this person see this article?”, one viewer at a time. A course asks the harder question — “is there any source in this course that some member of the intended audience may not see?” That is a quantified query over the same facts, and it is why the check lives in the formal layer rather than in a request handler.

Section 5

Meaning — the guardrail policy added here

Everything that requires understanding a text or looking at an image is configured in a managed guardrail service and versioned outside the application. A compliance officer states a forbidden topic as a definition, not as a word list; the service handles the paraphrase, the indirection, and the picture.

guardrail course-generator-guardrail version g3 // own history, own audit, changed without a deploy owner compliance denied topics stated as definitions in natural language personal data detect and block: email · phone · national id image policy evaluated on every generated image applied at 5 evaluation points — brief in · outline out · node out · image prompt out · image out node text and its images are screened in one call; the prompt leaving for the provider is screened inside generation unreachable fail closed: the run stalls, nothing is published

Two properties of the verdict matter more than its content. It enters the system as a fact for the formal layer — the guardrail decides nothing on its own; a state transition consumes its verdict alongside the other layers' proofs. And its version is stamped onto whatever it approved, which is what makes Act VI possible.

Why the hop is explicit

The platform talks to the model through the Anthropic Messages API rather than the AWS SDK, so there is no transparent wrapper that could apply a guardrail invisibly on the way past. The evaluation has to be its own call.

source: the platform's model client — Anthropic Messages API over Bedrock

That constraint turned out to be an advantage. An invisible guardrail is a guardrail you cannot point to in a trace; an explicit hop is a state in the machine, a row in the audit, and a thing that can be shown to fail closed. Four of the five evaluation points occupy three states in the model — BriefValidation, OutlineGuardrail and OutputGuardrail, the last of which screens a node's text and its images together — they are there because the call is real, not because a diagram wanted a box. The fifth screens the image prompt on its way to the provider, and it sits inside the generation step rather than at a state of its own — the only outbound point, and the reason the count is five.

Section 6

Rules and numbers

What is checked before a source enters the context added here

Retrieved passages are quoted as data and never executed as instructions, so an instruction buried in an internal document is inert by construction. That is the architectural defense and it holds — but it is silent. A source is therefore also screened on the way in, for personal data and for instruction-shaped content, and a hit is recorded rather than merely neutralized. The difference matters at review time: without the screen the system is resistant to a planted instruction; with it, the system also knows it was tried, and that is the signal a person needs.

Who counts as “compliance” exists

The owner column above names compliance, and the platform has no such role — three organizational roles, none of them a compliance officer. Rather than add one, the capability is a permission granted to an existing role, which the role model already supports. The consequence is worth stating precisely rather than glossing: the audit records the person and the permission they exercised, so a change reads as “made by someone holding the policy permission” — accurate and traceable — but never as a role label. Ownership stays distinguishable; it just is not spelled with a job title.

The policy bundle added here

Structural rules — the ones stated about graphs, sets, orderings and roles — are an OPA bundle with its own version. They answer questions the guardrail cannot, and they answer them with a reason rather than a score.

may_author(editor, audience) // role and scope required_sections(course_type) // what a compliance course must contain max_audience_breadth(source_class) // how far a restricted source may travel approval_chain(course_type) // who signs, and in what order

Thresholds added here

The numbers the state machine obeys are organizational data, for the same reason as everything else on this page: an organization that wants three repair attempts instead of two should not need a release. This is also why the repair budget is a parameter rather than a constant — see retry_budget_left.

{ "repair_budget": 2, // retries before BlockedRecoverable "max_nodes_per_course": 40, "max_minutes_per_lesson": 15, // feeds the Z3 feasibility check "reader_grace_days": 30, // how long a learner keeps a superseded // revision — and therefore how far the // staleness sweep has to reach "inherit_node_approvals": true // unchanged nodes are not re-reviewed in // a forked revision. The publication // signature itself is NOT a setting — // see section 9 }

A threshold change applies forward only. Work already approved was approved under the versions stamped on it, which is the entire point of stamping; a tightened repair budget does not un-approve anything. The exception is not really an exception: if a change makes an existing course actually violate a rule, that is a policy version change, and the staleness machinery already handles it.

reader_grace_days is the entry worth pausing on: it looks like a courtesy to learners and is in fact the only thing bounding the cost of a policy sweep. A revision past its window has no readers, so it leaves the sweep on its own — the number an organization picks here is the number of revisions its compliance changes will have to re-verify.

Note what the second and third entries do: they are not enforcement, they are inputs to the solver. The feasibility refusal in step 3 is computed against them, which is why the same code refuses a different brief for a different organization.

Section 7

Models — the routing table extended here

The platform already routes between two models — a default and a cheaper, latency-sensitive one — plus a transport flag, all as deployment settings resolved per call.

source: deployment settings plus a per-call model resolver with a “light” flag

The generator needs the same idea one level up: routing by task, not by a boolean, and visible as data rather than as environment.

outline drafting → default // structure, worth the stronger model node content → default repair pass → default // a repair that fails twice is expensive summarization → light metadata, titles → light

The table is visible in the trace and not on the approval screen. Which model wrote a paragraph has to be answerable when something is being investigated; putting it in front of the editor at the moment of approval invites an argument about routing instead of about content.

Whatever the table says, the resolved model_id and version are stamped onto every node — so “which model wrote this paragraph, under which policy” is answerable six months later, and a model swap is a stamp change that the staleness cascade can act on like any other.

Section 8

How configuration binds to an artifact

Configuration would be a liability rather than a feature if changing it silently changed the meaning of everything already approved. It does not, because approval writes down what it was approved against.

every node and course carries: policy_version which OPA bundle judged it guardrail_version which managed policy cleared it catalog_version which block vocabulary validated it model_id + version which model produced it kb_snapshot which state of the sources it was grounded against threshold_set which numbers its budgets came from trace_id where its full history is approvals[] who signed, when, and what they were shown

Changing configuration therefore touches no course at all. What it does is make one query answerable: which artifacts were approved against a version that no longer exists? The dependency graph answers it, and only those courses move to StaleReview. Everything else keeps running, which is the difference between governance and an outage.

Versions are stamped per source, not as one bundle number. A single stamp would be simpler to store and much blunter to use: a catalog change would implicate every course whose policy version also moved, and the sweep would lose most of its precision. Five fields buy a cascade that only ever touches what actually changed.

A stamp is a claim about the past, not a lock on the future. It does not prevent the rule from changing — it makes the consequence of the change computable, and small.

Immutability is what makes the claim hold. Because a published revision is never edited, its stamps stay true for as long as it exists: the versions recorded on it are the versions it was actually judged under, not the versions it was judged under before someone amended it. That is also why rollback is a pointer move — an earlier revision is still intact and still carries its own stamps, so returning to it restores a known, explainable state rather than an approximation of one.

Section 9

What is deliberately not configuration

A page arguing that most things are data owes an equally explicit list of what is not — otherwise “configurable” quietly becomes “defeatable”.

  • The two state machines and their transitions. Which states exist and which moves are legal is the architecture, not a setting. An organization may tune a budget; it may not invent a path from ContentDrafting to Published.
  • The temporal invariants. That an exam follows its topics, that every generated artifact passed a guardrail, that a published course has no stale node — these are checked over the trace and cannot be waived by anyone.
  • Fail-closed behaviour. No configuration value turns an unreachable guardrail into a pass. The absence of a verdict is never a permissive verdict.
  • The boundary itself. What may be configured is not, in turn, configurable. There is no setting that moves a decision from the formal layer to the model.
  • The publication signature. An organization decides who signs and how many signatures a course type needs; it does not decide whether a revision is signed at all. Node approvals are inherited by a fork so unchanged work is not re-reviewed — the signature under publication never is.
  • The visual-review requirement. An organization may decide which block types carry the flag; it may not switch the flag off wholesale. It exists precisely for what no automated layer can close, so a setting that waives it would waive the only check an image ever gets.
  • The audit record. Append-only, not a preference, and not something an admin can shorten.
  • The order of evaluation. Cheap and deterministic first, the model last. This is a property of the gateway, not a tuning knob — an organization that could reorder it could make itself pay for every refusal.

The rule that keeps the list honest: configuration is the seam through which an organization changes behaviour without a release. Anything that would let configuration remove a guarantee is not configuration.

Section 10

When configuration fails

Every source above is a dependency, and dependencies fail. What matters is that each failure has one obvious safe answer.

FailureWhat happensWhy that is right
The guardrail service is unreachable The gateway fails closed. The run stalls in ErrorRecovery; nothing reaches an editor and nothing is published The absence of a verdict is not a permissive verdict
A block type is withdrawn from the catalog Published revisions are immutable, so they keep rendering under their stamped catalog_version; new generation cannot propose the type; affected revisions go to StaleReview and any repair happens in a fork A withdrawal is a policy change like any other — it must be reviewed, not applied retroactively in silence. Entries are deprecated, never hard-deleted: a stamp that cannot be resolved is a published course that can no longer be explained
Two policy rules contradict each other Caught when the bundle is loaded, not when a course is generated An unsatisfiable bundle would refuse every course with an incomprehensible reason. Better to refuse the bundle
Configuration changes mid-run The run finishes against the versions it started with; the new versions apply to the next run and to the staleness sweep A single run must be judged against one consistent world, or its trace cannot be replayed
The Knowledge Base moved under a grounded node The node's kb_snapshot no longer matches; it becomes NeedsRevalidation Grounding was a claim about a specific state of the sources, not about the sources in general
An admin sets a threshold that makes generation impossible The Z3 pre-flight refuses the brief with an unsat core naming the threshold The refusal should point at the setting that caused it, not at the author who tripped over it

Section 12

Open questions

Both are decisions for the organization rather than gaps in the design: the machinery to answer either way is already described above, and neither answer changes a record shape or an owner. They are the reason this page is marked reviewed rather than final.

  1. How finely does the policy permission split? Compliance capability is a permission on an existing role, which works — but “may change the guardrail policy” and “may change the thresholds” are different powers with different blast radii, and granting them as one bundle makes the smaller one as heavy as the larger.
  2. Should reader_grace_days vary by course type? A thirty-day tail is generous for onboarding and questionable for a compliance course, where the whole point is that everyone is on the current rules. Varying it means the threshold stops being one number and starts being a policy — which may be the honest answer.
